Abstract

During the Great Recession, labor markets often exhibit a slow unemployment recovery and persistent outward shifts in the Beveridge curve, which suggests a decline in the efficiency of the job-matching process. While it is often explained by worker search intensity, we argue that the direction of search behavior also matters by proposing a stylized theoretical model based on the Free Energy Principle. Through modeling agents who actively divide their effort between applying for jobs and learning about the market's new state, our framework shows that agents endogenously shift effort from applications to learning when their uncertainty is high. Building on this micro-foundation, we design a macroeconomic model where matching efficiency is no longer an external parameter but is instead governed by two cognitive factors: the share of unemployed workers with misaligned beliefs and the average learning effort of the informed. Simulation results show that a structural shock will divert effort to learning and depress matching by creating widespread uncertainty, and the subsequent slow recovery is governed by the realignment of collective beliefs.

The puzzle

Search effort explains how hard people look. It does not explain where they look.

Severe downturns push the Beveridge curve outward and leave it there: for any given number of vacancies, unemployment stays higher than it used to be. Something about the matching process itself has become less efficient. In the canonical Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides framework that efficiency is a parameter — exogenous, or hit by an ad hoc shock. This paper asks what it is made of.

The standard measure

Intensity

Time spent searching, applications sent. A natural proxy, and the one the literature has traditionally focused on — but individual effort can be high while aggregate matching still falls.

What the evidence points to

Effectiveness

Shifts in the composition of the unemployed pool can mechanically reduce the aggregate matching rate. Mismatch unemployment — where seekers' attributes do not align with what jobs demand — means the problem is not a lack of effort but a misdirection of it.

This paper

Direction

Misdirection follows from Knightian uncertainty. After a structural shock, agents do not face known probabilities over known states — their model of the economy is simply wrong, and they must first find out what the world now is.

The three questions. (1) How can a job seeker deal with the trade-off between job applications and information gathering? (2) How do these micro-level cognitive processes aggregate to shape the macroeconomic matching function? (3) How does this endogenous cognitive channel influence the labor market's dynamic response to structural shocks?
Theoretical framework

One objective for both believing and acting

The Free Energy Principle supplies a single quantity that governs belief updating and action selection. Its appeal here is parsimony: the drive to gather information is not assumed, priced, or given a capacity constraint. It falls out of the objective.

Perception · minimise F

Beliefs are chosen to bound surprise

An agent cannot minimise surprise −log p(s) directly, because that requires the marginal likelihood. It instead minimises variational free energy, a tractable upper bound. Rearranged, that bound is a familiar trade-off.

(4) F= DKL [q(θ)|| p(θ)] − 𝔼q(θ) [logp (s|θ)]
Complexity
How far the posterior strays from the prior. An Occam's razor that keeps the model from chasing noise.
Accuracy
How well the beliefs explain what was actually observed.

Action · minimise G

Actions are chosen to bound future surprise

Under active inference the agent selects the action whose anticipated outcomes carry the least expected free energy. That quantity splits into two motives an economist will recognise.

(6) G(at)= 𝔼q(o) [−logp(o)] + 𝔼q(o,s) [logq(s|o) −logq(s|a)]
Pragmatic value
The divergence between expected outcomes and the agent's preferences. Formally analogous to maximising expected utility — here, getting a job.
Epistemic value
The expected reduction in uncertainty about the hidden state. This term rewards exploration and has no counterpart in expected utility theory.
Why not rational inattention? RI posits an exogenous cost function or a fixed processing capacity, so information is valuable only instrumentally. Active inference imposes no explicit information cost: the pull toward information emerges from the imperative to minimise prediction error, giving information intrinsic value. The paper's framework is about the epistemic value of information rather than its price.
Section 3 · the worker

One unit of effort, two things to spend it on

A worker faces a market in one of two latent states: an old economy where traditional skills are in demand, or a new economy that wants a different skill set. She cannot observe which. Each period she splits a unit of effort: a fraction α goes to finding out which world she is in, and 1−α goes to applying for jobs. Applications directed at the wrong world mostly fail.

Epistemic · fraction α

Learning

Reading industry reports, attending networking events, researching where skill demand is heading. Pays nothing today; it changes which direction tomorrow's applications point.

Pragmatic · fraction 1−α

Applying

Submitting applications. Success depends on both the intensity of the effort and whether the belief steering it happens to be right.

The prediction. When the belief distribution has high entropy, the epistemic value of information is substantial and the agent raises α. When entropy is low the epistemic drive fades and effort flows back to applications. Nothing in the agent says "learn when confused" — it is what minimising expected free energy implies.

How this simulator relates to the paper

It is the authors' code. This is a port of the simulation notebook, not a reconstruction from the appendix prose. The likelihood matrices, the preference vector C = log[0.1, 0.8, 7, 0.8, 7, 1, 1], the transition matrix, the partial belief update, the sampled discrete action and the softmax over expected free energy all follow it line for line. At the published settings it reproduces the figures closely: belief 0.97 → 0.87 → 0.14, α spanning roughly 0.12–0.72, entropy 0.10–0.68 nats.

Two statistics, not one. The paper reports a correlation of 0.928 between effort and uncertainty. That is computed on the averaged curves — 60 points after pooling 100 runs. The correlation across the raw 100×60 sample is only 0.63, and the notebook prints exactly that. Both are shown above. The gap is not a discrepancy; averaging removes the per-run sampling noise, and it is worth knowing which of the two a reader is looking at.

Why the single run looks so different. The policy samples a discrete action, so in one run α only ever takes the values 0.1, 0.5 or 0.9 — the smooth curve in Figure 1 is an artefact of averaging, not something any individual worker does. Switching modes above makes this visible: the scatter panel collapses from a cloud to three vertical stripes.

Belief inertia is doing the work. The agent is not a full Bayesian. Each step it moves only 40% of the way from its prediction toward the evidence-based posterior. That single parameter, rather than any noisiness in the observations, is what makes belief revision take about twenty steps instead of three. Push the slider to 1.0 and the agent flips almost immediately.

Three things in the code worth knowing about. The belief update uses the previous step's alpha and applied direction while the observation was generated with the current ones — a one-step offset. The update also switches likelihood hard at alpha > 0.5, whereas action selection blends the two channels continuously. And the environment is slightly harsher than the agent's own model of it: a mismatched application always returns "no offer", while the agent believes it might still yield a low offer 10% of the time. All three are reproduced here as-is, since they are what generated the published figures.

One thing the code settles. Equation (6) as printed adds the epistemic term to G, which — since G is minimised — would make the agent avoid information. The notebook computes efe = −(pragmatic_value + info_gain), rewarding it. The sign in the manuscript appears to be a slip; the implementation is the one Section 2.2 describes in words.

About these three figures. They are the paper's Figures 1–3, but redrawn. The data comes from running the authors' simulation notebook unchanged — 100 runs, 60 steps, shock at 30, learning_rate = 0.4, β = 10, numpy seed 0 — and keeping the same averaged series the published figures plot. Section 4 · the market

Matching efficiency, taken apart

Aggregate a continuum of these agents and the efficiency parameter stops being a parameter. Partition the unemployed by whether their beliefs match the true state, assume that misdirected applications essentially never land, and the job-finding rate factorises into a familiar tightness term and two cognitive ones.

(13) ft= (1−λt) ⋅ (1− α¯tT )⋅ f¯(θt)
Composition · 1 − λ
The share of the unemployed whose search is pointed at the right world. Everyone else is searching effectively in vain.
Behaviour · 1 − ᾱ
How much of the correctly informed group's effort is actually going into applications rather than into learning.
Tightness · f̄(θ)
The standard DMP term, m₀ θ1−γ, unchanged.

The first two collapse into a single cognitive matching channel:

(14) μt= (1−λt) ⋅ (1− α¯tT ) ⏟ cognitive matching channel  Cₜ ⋅μ¯

Matching efficiency is no longer a constant: it now fluctuates with the collective beliefs and uncertainty of the workforce.

Where misalignment comes from, and goes

The share of the unemployed holding false beliefs moves under three forces, derived in Appendix C from the population flows.

(16) λt˙= (λe−λt) sEtUt +(fT−fF) λt(1−λt) −kλt
Inflow Newly separated workers arrive carrying whatever share of false beliefs λe the employed had, pulling the pool toward it.
Selection Correctly informed workers leave for jobs faster, so the misinformed concentrate among those who remain. This term works against recovery.
Learning Misinformed workers gather information, discover their model is wrong, and switch. The rate k is the speed of collective cognitive adjustment.

Uncertainty is measured by disagreement

Aggregating the individual policy exactly would mean integrating over the whole distribution of belief states. Instead the paper carries the micro insight up directly: average learning effort rises with the uncertainty prevailing in the market, and the natural measure of that is the entropy of the split between the two belief groups.

(17) α¯tT= αbase+c ⋅H(λt)

Entropy is minimal when the workforce agrees — whether it is collectively right (λ→0) or collectively wrong (λ→1) — and maximal at λ=0.5, where nobody knows which world they are in. Drag along the curve.

One place where the code and the text differ. Section 4 assumes misdirected applications are systematically unsuccessful, so that fF ≈ 0 and the job-finding rate factorises cleanly into (1−λ)(1−ᾱ)f̄(θ). The code keeps fF = 0.02, so the actual series are (1−λ)(1−ᾱ)f̄ + λfF. The difference is small but it is why the job-finding rate never quite collapses to the cognitive channel alone.

And one quirk worth knowing. In the loop the shock is applied after the period's derived quantities have been computed, so λ jumps at t = 50 while entropy, effort, C, f and u only respond at t = 51. That one-step lag is reproduced here. Note also that entropy is in bits in this section (log2, Equation 18) but in nats in the micro figures — which is why H peaks near 1 here and near 0.69 there.

Section 5 · policy

Two margins, and a trade-off between them

If the bottleneck after a structural change is cognitive, then policy has two places to push: the speed at which beliefs realign, and the intensity of the learning detour that misalignment provokes.

Raise k

Accelerate belief alignment

Acts directly on the learning flow that drains the stock of misinformed searchers. Timely guidance and counselling; modular re-skilling and certification with fast feedback; job-search assistance coordinated with local demand signals.

Lower c

Improve the information environment

Helps correctly informed workers waste less effort on epistemic activity even when market-wide disagreement is high. Richer labor-market information; clearer vacancy–skill taxonomies; interoperable credentials; platforms that surface relevant matches.

They are complementary, but not free. Faster learning shortens the misalignment phase; better information lowers the intensity of the detour itself. Interventions that raise learning can temporarily pull effort away from applications — so cost-effectiveness, and targeting the groups with the highest misalignment, both matter.
Discussion

What the mechanism predicts, and what it cannot yet do

Evidence consistent with the channel

  • Information-seeking is measurable and cyclical. Google job-search indices improve unemployment forecasting and spike around labor market turns; application-level data show systematic patterns in search intensity over an unemployment spell.
  • Re-skilling is countercyclical. U.S. community college and higher-education enrolment rose notably during and after the Great Recession.
  • Information-intensive ALMPs work, especially in recessions. Job-search assistance and training tend to have positive medium-run effects and perform relatively well when the market is weak.

Each is what one would expect if, after a shock that changes the structure of demand, workers divert effort from applications to belief updating — depressing effective matches until beliefs realign.

Limitations the authors name

  • Homogeneous agents. No heterogeneity in priors, learning ability or risk preferences.
  • One-sided. Only the worker infers. Firms do not learn about the quality or composition of the labor pool, and there is no vacancy-posting decision.
  • Qualitative. The framework is a stylized proof-of-concept; the parameters have not been calibrated to data, and the predictions have not been tested against it.

Planned next steps run along two streams: an econometric one, to calibrate the novel parameters and test the predictions against labor market data; and a macroeconomic one, for formal comparative statics and business cycle analysis.

The claim, stated plainly. The speed of labor market recovery may be constrained not only by aggregate demand, but by the slow and costly process of collective cognition. A structural shock does not merely destroy jobs — it invalidates what a large number of people believe about where the jobs are, and rebuilding that takes time that no amount of individual effort can compress.
