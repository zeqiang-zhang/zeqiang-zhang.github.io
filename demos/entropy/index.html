Abstract

During the Great Recession, labor markets often exhibit a slow unemployment recovery and persistent outward shifts in the Beveridge curve, which suggests a decline in the efficiency of the job-matching process. While it is often explained by worker search intensity, we argue that the direction of search behavior also matters by proposing a stylized theoretical model based on the Free Energy Principle. Through modeling agents who actively divide their effort between applying for jobs and learning about the market's new state, our framework shows that agents endogenously shift effort from applications to learning when their uncertainty is high. Building on this micro-foundation, we design a macroeconomic model where matching efficiency is no longer an external parameter but is instead governed by two cognitive factors: the share of unemployed workers with misaligned beliefs and the average learning effort of the informed. Simulation results show that a structural shock will divert effort to learning and depress matching by creating widespread uncertainty, and the subsequent slow recovery is governed by the realignment of collective beliefs. Our work provides a cognitive explanation for this observed persistence of unemployment and the shift of the Beveridge curve.

Model

After a structural change, job seekers do not know which state the labor market is in. Each worker divides a unit of effort between gathering information about the market (a share α) and applying for jobs (1 − α). Under active inference, the worker chooses the allocation that minimizes expected free energy, which combines a pragmatic term (finding a job) with an epistemic term (reducing uncertainty about the market state). Applications based on wrong beliefs rarely succeed.

Aggregating these workers, matching efficiency becomes endogenous. It is determined by the share λt of unemployed workers with misaligned beliefs and the average information-gathering effort ᾱt of the informed. The share λt changes through the inflow of newly separated workers, the faster job finding of informed workers, and learning at rate k. The average effort rises with collective uncertainty, measured by the entropy of the belief split, with sensitivity c.

Results

Individual worker

When the market state changes, the worker's belief in the old state declines gradually and effort shifts from applications to information gathering. Effort returns to applications once beliefs have adjusted. Across periods, information-gathering effort rises with the entropy of the worker's beliefs.

Labor market

A shock that raises the share of misinformed workers increases collective uncertainty and draws effort away from applications. The cognitive matching channel and the job-finding rate drop, and unemployment rises and returns slowly as beliefs realign. Because lower matching efficiency shifts the Beveridge curve outward, the model links this slow recovery to a persistent outward shift of the curve.

Policy

The model points to two policy margins. Accelerating belief alignment (higher k), for example through counselling and fast-feedback retraining, shortens the misalignment phase. Improving the information environment (lower c), for example through clearer vacancy–skill information, reduces the effort that informed workers spend on learning. Both lower steady-state unemployment and shorten the recovery.
